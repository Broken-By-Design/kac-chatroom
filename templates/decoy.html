Chapter 14 | The Rise of Industrial America

14.2 The Second Industrial Revolution

Railroads, steel, and electricity remake the American economy, 1865-1900.

The years between the end of the Civil War and the close of the nineteenth century brought the greatest economic expansion in American history. In 1865 the United States was a nation of farms, small workshops, and local trade. By 1900 it produced more manufactured goods than Great Britain and Germany combined. Railroads, steel, and electricity were the forces behind that change.

Railroads made the new industrial economy possible. Congress authorized a transcontinental line during the Civil War, and in May 1869 the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific met at Promontory Summit, Utah. The track they laid, together with thousands of miles built in the following decades, gave the country about 35,000 miles of railroad in 1865 and nearly 193,000 miles by 1900. Freight rates fell, distant regions were tied to national markets, and towns along the lines grew into cities. The railroads also created enormous demand for iron, steel, coal, and labor.

Steel was the signature material of the era. The Bessemer process, developed in the 1850s by Henry Bessemer in England and William Kelly in the United States, forced air through molten iron to burn off impurities and produce steel cheaply. Later open-hearth furnaces made the metal more reliable still. Andrew Carnegie built his steel empire around these methods at Homestead, Pennsylvania, outside Pittsburgh. He bought the mines, furnaces, and mills he needed, and he pushed his plants to run at full capacity. In 1901 Carnegie sold his company to J. P. Morgan, who combined it with other firms to create United States Steel, the first corporation in the world worth more than one billion dollars.

Electricity changed both the factory and the home. Thomas Edison opened his laboratory at Menlo Park, New Jersey, in 1876, developed a practical incandescent light bulb in 1879, and began supplying electric power to customers in New York City in 1882. Electric motors allowed factories to run without steam engines or water power, so manufacturing spread through the cities. Streetcars powered by electricity redrew the map of American cities, and by the 1890s electric machinery was common in textile mills, print shops, and machine works.

The cost of the new machinery favored large companies. John D. Rockefeller used horizontal integration, buying one refinery after another, to bring most of the nation's oil refining under the control of Standard Oil. Carnegie practiced vertical integration, owning the raw materials, the transportation, and the mills his steel required. Leaders of industry argued that size brought efficiency and lower prices. Their critics replied that trusts and monopolies crushed competition. Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890 to outlaw combinations in restraint of trade, but for its first decade the law was rarely enforced.

Industrial work was hard and often dangerous. Most factory hands labored ten to twelve hours a day, six days a week, for wages that left little to spare. In the 1870s and 1880s workers responded by organizing. The Knights of Labor, founded in 1869, admitted both skilled and unskilled workers, men and women, and white and Black members. Its membership grew quickly and then fell after the Haymarket affair in Chicago in 1886. That same year Samuel Gompers helped found the American Federation of Labor, a union of skilled workers that used strikes and collective bargaining to press for higher wages and shorter hours. The Homestead strike of 1892 and the Pullman strike of 1894 ended in defeat for the unions, but they made clear that the conflict between labor and capital would be a central fact of American life.

Key Terms

  • Bessemer process: a method of making steel by forcing air through molten iron to remove impurities.
  • Horizontal integration: gaining control of an industry by buying out or merging with competing firms.
  • Vertical integration: controlling every stage of production, from raw materials to finished goods.
  • Trust: an arrangement in which the stockholders of competing companies turn their shares over to a single board of trustees.
  • Sherman Antitrust Act: an 1890 federal law that declared illegal any combination in restraint of trade.

Review Questions

  2. Why did steel replace iron as the main building material of the late nineteenth century?
  3. Compare horizontal integration with vertical integration, using Standard Oil and Carnegie Steel as examples.
  4. What caused the growth of labor unions in the 1880s, and what obstacles did they face?
